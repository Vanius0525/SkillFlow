\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Skills supplied in context let language models use formulas, rules and
step-by-step instructions without updating their weights. This is relevant
to the Web in two ways. Web search and retrieval deliver skill-bearing
material whose representation can affect answer quality
\citep{tan2025htmlrag}. Web agents also inject reusable skills and workflows
to guide browser tasks: Agent Workflow Memory supplies routines derived from
past trajectories, while WebXSkill couples action programs with step-level
guidance \citep{wang2024awm, wang2026webxskill}. These systems make skill
injection and reuse a concrete Web problem. Beyond selecting a useful skill,
we need to understand how its injected content supports computation:
\emph{when can internal skill states substitute for their source, and when
must that source remain accessible?} We study this question using
known-correct skill documents and controlled tasks.

Our experiments expose an apparent paradox. Skills substantially improve
answers, and their early hidden states can transfer most of that benefit to
a run containing an unrelated skill. Yet this transfer effect collapses well
before the final layer. On Qwen3-8B and clinical calculators, span replacement
recovers $0.74$ of the available gain at layer $12$ but only $0.16$ at layer
$13$ of $36$. What has changed? The model may have finished using the
skill, or the skill may remain useful while its states have become
ineffective in the receiving computation. Existing evidence that compact
states transfer tasks and instructions \citep{hendel2023taskvectors,
todd2024functionvectors, davidson2025prompting, bigoulaeva2026instructionvectors}
does not distinguish these interpretations of a failed transfer.

The decisive experiment tests both interpretations with complementary
interventions. We first replace the skill-span states of an equal-length
wrong-skill receiver with states from the correct-skill run. We then block
all subsequent attention to the skill in the original correct-skill run.
The first intervention measures \emph{transferability}; the second measures
\emph{continued reading dependence}. Varying their starting layer reveals
whether loss of transfer coincides with tolerance to losing source access.
To make the transfer test informative, we first establish a content-sensitive
probe: matched subtraction isolates the correct-versus-wrong skill
difference, and early span replacement recovers $0.89$ of the gain on a
screened $135$-item subset, whereas final-position replacement is ineffective
for the same clinical reasoning task.

Our core discovery is that \emph{loss of transfer precedes reduced dependence
on reading}. Transfer falls below half of the available gain at layer $13$,
but blocking further skill attention leaves fewer than half of the rescued
items correct until layer $25$. Between these boundaries, the source remains
important to its original computation even though transplanting its states
has little effect in the receiver. The paradox is therefore resolved at the
level of what the interventions measure: transfer failure does not establish
that the model has finished using the source. This separation gives document
representation and reuse two distinct evaluation targets, rather than a
single notion of whether a skill has been internalised.

Further experiments test the scope and alternative explanations of this
finding. The boundary ordering holds in all six evaluated combinations of
three models and two task domains, using each intervention's eligible
subset, and persists on their common eligible items. Repeated late replacement does not recover the early transfer
effect. Content, position and cross-question controls establish that
early recovery depends on structured skill content. We make three
contributions:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\parskip0pt
\item \textbf{A test separating transferability from source dependence.}
Complementary state replacement and attention knockout reveal an intermediate
depth interval of weak transfer and strong reading dependence
(\S\ref{sec:depth}).
\item \textbf{A validated probe of skill content.} Matched content,
position and cross-question controls identify what supports early recovery
rather than an arbitrary perturbation
(\S\ref{sec:battery}, \S\ref{sec:whose}).
\item \textbf{Evidence for the separation's scope and boundaries.} Cross-model
and cross-task replication, common-item checks and repeated replacement
constrain the scope and simple explanations of the depth gap
(\S\ref{sec:depth}, \S\ref{sec:ladder}).
\end{enumerate}
