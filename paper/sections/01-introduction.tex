\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Web search increasingly supplies documents directly to language models, which
use retrieved content to answer questions and carry out tasks. This makes
document delivery part of the computation: systems must decide what content
to retain and what can be replaced by a shorter representation. Work on
Web retrieval-augmented generation already shows that the representation of
retrieved material affects its usefulness \citep{tan2025htmlrag}.
Procedural documents pose a particular challenge because formulas, conditions
and ordered steps must support computation beyond retrieving a fact.
\emph{When can the model's internal representation stand in for a supplied
procedure, and when does successful computation still require access to its
source tokens?} This is the reader-side question we study, motivated by the
use of external procedures in retrieval-augmented Web systems. We isolate it
with controlled procedural tasks and known-correct documents, holding
retrieval fixed.

We call a reusable procedure, formula, lookup table or worked example supplied
in context a \emph{skill}. Skills can substantially improve performance
\citep{su2026srabench, skillsbench2026}, but the improvement does not reveal
whether the model has formed a reusable representation or still relies on
reading the document. Compact states can transfer tasks and instructions
\citep{hendel2023taskvectors, todd2024functionvectors,
davidson2025prompting, bigoulaeva2026instructionvectors}, with effectiveness
depending on position and task scope \citep{jit2025taskrepr}. A failed transfer,
however, has several possible causes: the probe may miss useful states,
the receiving computation may be unable to use them, or the source may no
longer matter. Distinguishing these possibilities is essential to interpreting
what a context representation does.

Our central finding is a separation between \emph{transferability} and
\emph{continued reading dependence}. We transplant the skill's states into a
run with an unrelated, equal-length skill, then separately block attention to
the skill in a correct-skill run. On Qwen3-8B and MedCalc, transfer falls below
half of the available gain at layer $13$ of $36$, whereas blocking subsequent
reading still leaves fewer than half of the rescued items correct until layer
$25$. There is therefore a substantial intermediate depth interval in which
the states are difficult to transfer yet remain important to their original
computation. This ordering holds in all six evaluated combinations of three
models and two task domains. It rules out interpreting loss of transfer as
the point at which the model has finished using the source.

We establish an effective transfer probe before interpreting its failure.
Matched content subtraction separates the useful procedure from the effect
of adding a document. Synthetic answer-format comparisons expose the limits
of a final-position probe. Early span replacement then recovers $0.89$ of the
gain on a screened clinical-calculator subset. Content, cross-question,
position and rank controls identify what supports this recovery. Repeated
late replacement and spectral diagnostics constrain explanations of its loss.

For context compression and reuse, successful state transfer and safe
removal of source access become separate evaluation targets. We contribute:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\parskip0pt
\item \textbf{A paired test of transfer and reading.} Span replacement and
attention knockout reveal an intermediate regime of weak transfer and strong
source dependence, replicated across models and task domains
(\S\ref{sec:depth}--\ref{sec:ladder}).
\item \textbf{A controlled account of the transferred content.} Matched
subtraction and answer-format tests motivate span interventions. Content,
position and cross-question controls identify what makes early transfer
effective (\S\ref{sec:decomp}--\ref{sec:whose}).
\item \textbf{Constraints on representation compactness.} Rank
truncation, repeated late injection and spectral diagnostics distinguish
behaviourally useful structure from geometric summaries
(\S\ref{sec:rank}--\ref{sec:depth}).
\end{enumerate}
