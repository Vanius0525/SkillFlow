\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{Relevance to the Web and to this track.}
Web agents draw on skills to carry out tasks across sites and services.
Reusable workflows and skill guidance already help agents perform browser
tasks \citep{wang2024awm,wang2026webxskill}. As these agents retrieve and
share skills, a basic question arises: when can the internal states produced
by reading a skill support another execution? This concerns the reuse of
external knowledge in agentic information access, connecting our work to
the Search, Recommendation, and Retrieval-Augmented AI track. We study
this question through controlled model interventions on supplied skills.

Language-model agents increasingly gain capabilities by loading skills into
context. An Agent Skill packages task instructions, scripts and supporting
resources around a \texttt{SKILL.md} entry point, which an agent loads when
needed \citep{zhang2025agentskills}. We study the instruction content of a
loaded skill. A model may apply this content successfully, but how much of
that ability can be reused through its internal states? Reusing states from
an earlier execution is an appealing route to sharing skills and avoiding
repeated processing. It requires understanding what those states contribute
and what else they need in order to work.

There are two possibilities. The model may form states that recover the
skill's contribution when moved to another execution. Alternatively, those
states may work together with the computation that produced them and with
continued access to the skill. In the latter case, a useful source could
become hard to transfer before the model has finished using it. We therefore
ask: \emph{how does the ability to transfer a skill's states relate to the
ongoing need to read that skill?}

Task and function vectors show that internal states can transmit task
behaviour across contexts \citep{hendel2023taskvectors,todd2024functionvectors}.
Work on layer-wise computation and reusable skill caches further highlights
the roles of depth and execution history
\citep{yang2025internalcot,li2026prefillnotes}. We bring transfer and source
use into the same experimental design, following both across model depth.

We use two interventions (Figure~\ref{fig:two-probes}). First, we replace
the states at skill-token positions in a wrong-skill execution with states
from a correct-skill execution. This tests whether the transplanted states
recover the skill's benefit in the receiving computation. Second, we block
attention to the skill from a chosen layer onward in the original
correct-skill execution. This tests how much performance survives without
further source access. Both interventions vary the layer at which they
begin, allowing us to compare when transfer weakens and when source access
becomes less important.

Early state transfer provides an effective starting point. On a screened
Qwen3-8B/MedCalc set, replacing the skill span at layer $8$ recovers $89\%$
of the correct skill's accuracy gain. Matched content and position controls
show that this effect depends on structured skill information. When we
follow the interventions deeper, however, their effects separate. Transfer
first falls below half the available gain at layer $13$ of $36$, while
blocking subsequent skill attention remains strongly damaging until layer
$25$. This ordering appears across three models from two families and two
task domains, including comparisons on identical eligible questions.

The gap motivates a test of computational history. A state transplant leaves
the receiver's previously computed attention caches in place. Pairing skill
states with their corresponding cache entries restores substantial late
transfer. At layer $16$, this joint repair raises accuracy from $3.5\%$ to
$42.1\%$, but cutting subsequent reading reduces it to $7.0\%$. The repaired
states work with their cached history and further source access.

Our contributions follow this progression. First, we validate an early
skill-span transfer probe using content, position and cross-question controls
(\S\ref{sec:battery}). Second, we identify a depth interval in which transfer
is weak while the original execution still depends on skill attention
(\S\ref{sec:depth}). Third, we test how source caches and continued reading
support late transfer (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). Together, these findings
establish separate criteria for applying a contextual skill and reusing its
internal states in another execution.
