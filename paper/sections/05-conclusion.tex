\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Using a contextual skill and reusing its internal states are distinct
abilities. Our experiments reveal a
depth interval in which skill-state transfer has weakened while the original
computation continues to depend on skill attention. This separation recurs
across three models and two task domains, including common-item evaluation.

Content and cross-question controls establish an effective early probe.
Late transfer can be partly restored by pairing source states with their
cached history, and the recovered behaviour can still require continued
source reading. This connects transferability to the computation surrounding
the skill states. The mechanism study covers one model--task setting,
leaving the roles of history and reading in larger models and interactive
agent tasks for further study.

Contextual competence therefore has two distinct properties: the ability to
support the current computation and the ability to be reused in another.
For skill-sharing agents and context-reuse systems, both need to be tested.
Future skill-sharing systems can use paired transfer and source-access tests
to evaluate when an internal representation can substitute for the skill
that produced it.
