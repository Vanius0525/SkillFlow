\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:cells}

\paragraph{Tasks and models.}
We pair MedCalc-Bench \citep{khandekar2024medcalc} with skills from
SRA-Bench \citep{su2026srabench}, covering $1{,}100$ questions from $55$
clinical calculators. We also use SRA-Bench's TheoremQA subset, with $747$
questions and $320$ skills. Each question receives its designated skill,
holding skill selection fixed. We test Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-0.6B
\citep{yang2025qwen3} and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, spanning two model families. Models generate
step-by-step answers with greedy decoding and no tools, and we use the
corresponding benchmark scorers. On the full MedCalc benchmark, Qwen3-8B
achieves $74.2\%$ accuracy with the correct skill, $34.5\%$ without a skill,
and $31.5\%$ with an unrelated skill.

\paragraph{Evaluation populations.}
Our causal comparisons focus on questions rescued by the correct skill,
as defined in \S\ref{sec:formulation}. On MedCalc/Qwen3-8B, $467$ rescued
questions from $48$ calculators enter the span-intervention battery.
Retaining skill groups whose wrong-skill receiver solves no sampled item
gives $135$ questions from $14$ calculators. The depth sweeps use this
group screen, with separately eligible transfer and knockout items.
We also recompute both curves on their common item IDs. TheoremQA uses
the same screen, becoming item-level for skills with one question.
The history-repair study uses a separate fixed set described in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism}. These populations estimate effects conditional on
baseline skill benefit.

\paragraph{Implementation and uncertainty.}
State replacement occurs during prefill, the processing of prompt tokens
before answer generation. Cumulative attention knockout applies during both
prefill and decoding. An identity patch writes the receiver's own states
back and should reproduce its output. Correcting an attention-mask mismatch
between state capture and decoding restores exact agreement, including
all $467$ MedCalc identity checks. All main results use the corrected
implementation. Appendix~\ref{app:detail} specifies the intervention
protocol, and Appendix~\ref{app:limits} records the correction.
We estimate $95\%$ confidence intervals by resampling whole skill groups
with replacement, preserving dependence among questions using the same
skill. This skill-group bootstrap measures uncertainty across the sampled
groups rather than treating their questions as independent.

\subsection{Validating a probe of reusable skill content}
\label{sec:battery}

\paragraph{Early span replacement recovers the skill's contribution.}
Before comparing transfer across depth, we establish that the intervention
transmits useful behaviour. At layer $8$ on the screened MedCalc set,
replacing states at the skill's token positions recovers most of the
correct skill's benefit (Table~\ref{tab:probe}). Patching the last prompt
position or the final $16$ positions has a much smaller effect.
This locates an effective intervention in the skill span. Its larger
number of replaced states also makes it a higher-capacity intervention
than the final-position alternatives.

\paragraph{The effect depends on skill content and position.}
The unrelated-skill, permuted-position and noise controls in
Table~\ref{tab:probe} all yield little recovery. For the content comparison,
the correct and unrelated skills are patched over a common prefix length.
The correct prefix recovers $0.65$ of the available gain. A mean of
same-family skill differences recovers $0.12$ on its smaller eligible set
of $41$ items. Rescaling patched states to match the receiver's norm at
each position preserves $0.87$ recovery, close to the full-span result.
These comparisons identify skill-specific content and its arrangement as
important to early transfer. The group screen matters: other-skill and
noise controls are more effective in excluded groups, so the control
contrast describes the screened population.



\paragraph{Early transfer generalizes across questions using the same skill.}
\label{sec:whose}
In the main prompt order, the skill precedes the question. Causal attention,
which restricts a token to attending to earlier positions, prevents those
skill states from encoding the later question. We additionally place the
skill after the question and obtain donor states from other questions using
the same skill. On $151$ eligible items from $17$ calculators, these states
recover $0.98$ at layer $8$, close to $1.02$ for a donor answering the
current question. At layer $16$, the values separate to $0.29$ and $0.49$.
Early transfer therefore carries information shared across instances, with
greater question sensitivity later. Patching individual contiguous quarters
of the main skill span also reveals content structure: the formula quarter
gives the largest standalone recovery, $0.56$. Appendix~\ref{app:fullscale}
provides the additional content and prompt-order controls.

\subsection{Transfer fails before source dependence ends}
\label{sec:where}
\label{sec:depth}
\label{sec:ladder}

\paragraph{The two depth profiles separate.}
With an effective early probe established, we follow state transfer and
source-attention knockout across every layer. Figure~\ref{fig:transferreading}
shows the six model--task combinations. For Qwen3-8B/MedCalc, transfer
recovery is $0.74$ at layer $12$, falls to $0.16$ at $13$, and is at most
$0.07$ from layer $15$ onward. Yet cutting skill attention through layer
$23$ leaves only $0.03$--$0.12$ of rescued questions correct. Retention
reaches $0.25$ at layer $24$ and first crosses one half at layer $25$.
Throughout layers $13$--$24$, transfer recovers less than half the gain,
while removing further skill attention still loses more than half the
rescued answers.



The first crossing summarizes each curve. Qwen3-8B/MedCalc retention
briefly falls below half again at layer $26$ before rising from $27$
onward. We retain these fluctuations in the measured curves.
The main finding is the separation of the two intervention effects:
states become ineffective at correcting the receiving computation while
source access still supports the original one.

\paragraph{The ordering holds on common questions across settings.}
Original transfer and knockout runs can retain different eligible questions.
We therefore recompute the curves over the intersection of their item IDs,
using eligibility determined from unpatched baselines.
Table~\ref{tab:paireddepth} reports the resulting boundaries.
All six pairs match those from the original populations.
Skill-group bootstrap $95\%$ intervals for the reading-minus-transfer
boundary difference exclude zero in all six settings.
Changing the half-effect threshold to $0.4$ or $0.6$ also preserves the
ordering. The gap thus recurs across the two model families and both
task domains under common-item evaluation.

\paragraph{Repeated late replacement leaves transfer weak.}
The depth gap raises a practical question: can repeated replacement sustain
the transferred skill after a single write becomes ineffective? We replace
source-span states at every layer within consecutive windows, using the
same $135$ MedCalc items and wrong-skill receiver as the depth sweep.
Each layer receives the donor states captured at that layer.

Figure~\ref{fig:repeated} shows that recovery depends strongly on where
replacement begins. Windows starting in the early layers preserve much
of the skill's benefit, while later windows recover little. Extending
replacement from layer $16$ through the final layer gives $0.06$ recovery,
equal to replacing layer $16$ alone. By comparison, replacing every layer
from the start recovers $0.98$. Repeated writes therefore support effective
transfer when begun early, but adding them after the transfer boundary
does little to restore the skill's contribution. This shifts the next
question from how often the states are replaced to what accompanies them:
the receiver still retains its previously computed attention caches.



\subsection{Testing why late skill states fail to transfer}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\paragraph{Repairing source states and their cached history.}
A residual-state patch changes a block output while leaving previously
computed source-cache entries in place. To test whether these entries
matter, we compare source residual replacement ($S$), source key/value-cache
replacement ($C$), their joint replacement ($SC$), and $SC$ followed by
cutting reader-to-source attention from the next layer. A reader is a token
outside the skill span, such as a question or generated answer token.
The cache condition replaces source entries through the intervention
layer, keeping earlier reader computation fixed.

We evaluate layers $8,12,16,24$ on a fixed $124$-question set from
$14$ calculators with fresh baselines. Of these, $114$ questions are correct
with the donor and incorrect with both the wrong-skill and no-skill baselines.
Both panels of Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} use these same questions.
All $48$ own-state controls across four GPU shards reproduce output tokens
exactly. Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism} gives implementation and eligibility
details.



\paragraph{Joint repair restores substantial late transfer.}
At layer $16$, residual replacement answers $4/114$ questions correctly,
cache replacement answers $8/114$, and joint repair answers $48/114$.
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism-contrasts} reports the repair gain and its
uncertainty. The joint gain exceeds the sum of the individual gains
relative to the receiver, showing that the states and their cached history
contribute together. At layer $24$, joint repair still improves on residual
replacement ($25.4\%$ versus $0.9\%$), although its advantage over cache
replacement alone ($18.4\%$) is uncertain. Compatible source caches thus
recover part of the late transfer effect.

\paragraph{Recovered transfer still uses subsequent source reading.}
Following joint repair at layer $16$, cutting reader-to-source attention
reduces accuracy to $8/114$. Most of the repaired effect therefore relies
on continued reading. To identify what the original cumulative knockout
disrupts, we separately cut reader-to-source attention, source-to-source
attention, or both in the unmodified donor, during prefill and decoding.
At layer $24$, reader-only and combined cuts each leave $29/114$ questions
correct, while a source-self-only cut leaves $110/114$ correct.
Continued reader access accounts for the dominant tested dependence at
this layer. Earlier cuts at layers $8$ and $16$ damage both paths.

\paragraph{The repair effect varies with depth.}
After joint repair at layer $24$, a reader recut yields $27.2\%$ accuracy,
with no detected additional loss. The repaired-yet-reading-dependent
result is therefore supported at layer $16$ and varies across the tested
depths. The main repair gain is stable to generation-length diagnostics:
it remains $38.5$ points on the $96$ questions whose three baselines finish
without truncation. An adverse reassignment of truncated outputs still
leaves a $35.1$-point gain (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}).
Taken together, the experiments show that source history can restore late
transfer and that the recovered behaviour can still depend on reading
the skill.
