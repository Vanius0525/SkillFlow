\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:cells}

\paragraph{Tasks and models.}
Our main evaluation pairs MedCalc-Bench \citep{khandekar2024medcalc} with
skills from SRA-Bench \citep{su2026srabench}, covering $55$ clinical calculators
with $20$ questions each ($1{,}100$ items). Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3}
generates step-by-step reasoning using greedy decoding, without tools. We
evaluate its answers with the benchmark scorer. Accuracy is $74.2\%$ with the
correct skill, $34.5\%$ without a skill, and $31.5\%$ with a wrong skill.
We also test Qwen3-0.6B and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, and evaluate transfer to SRA-Bench's TheoremQA subset
($747$ questions and $320$ skills) using its prompt and scorer.

\paragraph{Sampling and uncertainty.}
The main MedCalc span experiments cover $467$ rescued items from $48$
calculators with at least two such items. Screening for groups whose receiver
solves no sampled item retains $135$ items from $14$ calculators. The main
depth sweep and span-quarter comparisons use this same subset.
TheoremQA uses the same screen, becoming item-level when a skill has only one
question. Intervals resample skill groups, with donor and receiver baselines
matched to each intervention's available items. Only complete reruns enter
curves. Appendix~\ref{app:fullscale} records earlier development samples.

\paragraph{Identity control.}
Writing the receiver's own states back should leave its output unchanged.
This identity control exposed a mismatch: capture omitted an attention mask
that decoding supplied, changing $108/1{,}766$ Qwen3-8B outcomes ($6.1\%$).
Matching the masks restores exact agreement in every corrected run, for
example on all $467$ MedCalc items. All main-text interventions use the correction. Appendix~\ref{app:limits} records
provenance and Appendix~\ref{app:detail} implementation details. Small numerical
discrepancies can propagate through long generations \citep{he2025nondeterminism}.

\subsection{Validating a probe of reusable skill content}
\label{sec:battery}

\paragraph{Changing the intervention positions restores recovery.}
We next apply Eq.~\ref{eq:inject} across the skill's own positions. At layer
$8$, span injection raises accuracy from $0.000$ to $0.815$ on the screened
$135$-item subset, recovering $0.89$ of the donor's gain
(Table~\ref{tab:big40}). Figure~\ref{fig:channels} compares this with writing
donor states at the final position, the final $16$ positions, or up to $256$
positions after the skill, using the same layer, items and receiver.
All positions outside the chosen intervention retain their receiver states.
On the same items, the last $1$, $16$ and up to $256$ positions recover
$0.03$, $0.06$ and $0.05$, respectively.

\paragraph{Dose, transfer, order and noise controls.}
Recovery changes nonlinearly with injection strength: $\alpha=0.5,0.6,1.0$
gives $0.04,0.53,0.89$ recovery, while doubling the dose reduces it to $0.77$.
To test content specificity, we
compare correct and other-skill injections over a common prefix length: the
correct prefix recovers $0.65$, whereas content from an unrelated skill
recovers $0.05$. A same-family mean control recovers $0.12$ on its $41$ eligible items.
Permuting positions also gives $0.05$, norm-matched Gaussian noise
gives $0.02$, and the identity control gives $0.00$. These controls show that
recovery depends on the injected content and its arrangement across positions,
rather than perturbation size alone.

\paragraph{Does the transferred information generalize across questions?}
\label{sec:whose}

A successful same-question patch could carry a task-specific answer rather
than reusable skill content. Table~\ref{tab:skillcontent} tests this
alternative by varying the injected section and the donor question. Of four contiguous span quarters, the
formula quarter gives the largest standalone recovery ($0.56$), compared with
$0.89$ for the full span. With the skill before the question, causal masking
prevents its states from depending on later question tokens: states are
identical across instances at equal prompt length and nearly identical
otherwise (cosine $\ge0.994$ over all $48$ calculators). Placing the skill after the question allows
such dependence. Even then, states from other instances of the same skill
recover $0.98$ at layer $8$, close to the current instance's $1.02$. At layer
$16$, the gap widens to $0.29$ versus $0.49$, while the instance-specific
remainder recovers little on its own. Early transfer therefore relies largely
on content shared across questions, with greater question dependence later.

Two completed corrected controls test whether a shared shift or a change in
state norm could explain recovery. Averaging content differences across skills
recovers only $0.03$ with the skill first ($n=135$) and $0.04$ with it last
($n=151$). Rescaling patched states to match the receiver's norm at each
position preserves $0.87$ recovery, compared with $0.89$ without rescaling.
In the corrected rerun, layer-$16$
recovery is $0.49$ with the skill last ($151$ items, $17$ calculators) and
$0.06$ with it first ($135$ items, $14$ calculators). These differently
screened subsets do not identify a prompt-order effect.


\subsection{Transfer fails before source dependence ends}
\label{sec:where}
\label{sec:depth}

The effective early span intervention gives us a probe with which to test
the paradox: does losing the transfer effect at later layers mean
the model no longer needs the skill? Figure~\ref{fig:transferreading}
tests this inference directly.

\paragraph{Transfer weakens while reading remains important.}
Figure~\ref{fig:transferreading} compares transfer through span replacement
with continued use through attention across all six model--task combinations.
We first examine Qwen3-8B on MedCalc. Every layer is measured,
using the same screened groups and each intervention's eligible items. Replacement
recovers $0.92$--$0.98$ through layer $7$, $0.89$ at $8$ and $0.74$ at $12$
of $36$, then $0.16$ at $13$ and at most $0.07$ from $15$ onward ($n=135$,
$14$ calculators). We define the crossing below $\rho=0.5$ as the \emph{transfer
boundary} (reported as transfer in Table~\ref{tab:replication}). The
log-probability of the donor's generated reasoning also drops between these
layers, suggesting that binary answer grading sharpens, but does not fully
explain, the transition (Appendix~\ref{app:fullscale}). To test continued
reading, \emph{cumulative attention knockout} blocks attention to skill
tokens at every layer from $L$ onward. On the $134$ rescued items of the same
subset, $0.03$--$0.12$ stay correct for $L\le23$. This rises to $0.25$ at
$24$, first passes half at $25$ ($0.58$) and reaches $0.94$ at $35$. The
transfer boundary therefore lies at $13/36=0.36$ of depth and the reading
boundary at $25/36=0.69$, using the same screen and restricting knockout
to its donor-correct items.
Writing the last prompt position instead recovers at most $0.06$ at any
layer. \textbf{The same source is hard to reuse in the receiving computation while
remaining useful to the ongoing correct computation.} Continued dependence
on skill attention thus outlasts successful transfer from the skill's states. Transfer measures recovery in a wrong-skill
receiver, whereas knockout measures dependence in a correct-skill run. The
separation identifies the intermediate regime operationally: from layers
$13$ through $24$, span replacement recovers less than half the gain and
blocking further reading leaves fewer than half the rescued items correct.
The first crossing is at layer $25$; retention briefly falls below half again
at $26$, then rises above it from $27$ onward. The boundary summarises the
curve rather than asserting a monotonic transition. Thus transfer loss precedes,
rather than demonstrates, tolerance to removal of source access.

\paragraph{The ordering survives common-item evaluation.}
The six depth sweeps already cover Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-0.6B and Mistral-7B on
MedCalc and TheoremQA. Their original eligibility criteria retain different
numbers of items for transfer and knockout. We therefore recompute both
curves on their intersection, without selecting on intervention outcomes.
All six first-crossing pairs remain unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:paireddepth}).
Skill-group bootstrap $95\%$ intervals for the reading-minus-transfer boundary
difference exclude zero in every combination. This supports the ordering
beyond the Qwen family and on common items. The ordering also persists at
thresholds $0.4$ and $0.6$.

\paragraph{Repeated replacement does not bridge the gap.}
To test whether late failure reflects intervening at too few layers, we replace span
states repeatedly within consecutive layer windows, using the battery's
receiver and the same $135$ items as the depth sweep. Windows $8$--$11$,
$12$--$15$, $14$--$19$ and $16$--$35$ recover $0.89$, $0.74$, $0.16$ and
$0.06$. The last equals layer $16$ alone. Writing every layer at once
recovers $0.98$, so the transplant itself remains effective when
it starts early enough. The identity control is exact on all $467$ items. On
TheoremQA ($99$ items, $75$ groups, with its battery's receiver), the same
windows recover $0.84,0.61,0.38,0.20$, the last again equal to layer $16$
alone. Repeated late injection therefore does not restore early-layer
recovery in these tests. This rules out a simple shortage-of-interventions explanation for the late
transfer failure in these tests.

\subsection{Testing why late skill states fail to transfer}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The depth gap raises a computation-history question. Replacing the source-span
residual leaves its previously computed attention caches and reader history
unchanged. We test whether repairing the source cache restores transfer,
and whether the restored effect still requires access to the source.



\paragraph{Source-state and cache repair.}
On Qwen3-8B and MedCalc, we compare source-residual replacement (S), source
key/value-cache replacement (C), their combination (SC), and SC followed by
blocking reader-to-source attention from the next layer. C replaces source
cache entries through the intervention layer; earlier reader history remains
fixed. The four conditions are evaluated at layers $8,12,16,24$ on a fixed
$124$-item set from $14$ calculators, with fresh no-skill, donor and receiver
baselines. Of these, $114$ items across all $14$ calculators are donor-correct
and incorrect under both receiver and no-skill baselines; both panels use
these identical items. All $48$ own-state controls across the four GPU shards
reproduce output tokens exactly. This design tests
whether repairing the source's cached contribution improves on residual
replacement alone, and whether any recovery uses subsequent source access.

\paragraph{Separate reading from source updates.}
A second comparison blocks only reader-to-source attention, only
source-to-source attention, or both, from layers $8,16,24$ onward during
prefill and decoding. Separating these paths identifies which component of
the original cumulative knockout produces the performance loss.

\paragraph{Repairing history restores substantial late transfer.}
At layer $16$, S alone solves $4/114$ items ($3.5\%$), C solves $8/114$
($7.0\%$), and SC solves $48/114$ ($42.1\%$). The prespecified SC-minus-S
contrast is $38.6$ percentage points, and the joint gain exceeds the sum of
the two individual gains by $31.6$ points. Source states and their cached
history therefore contribute jointly: weak residual transfer does not mean
that useful source information has disappeared. At layer $24$, SC still
improves on S ($25.4\%$ versus $0.9\%$), but no longer clearly exceeds C
alone ($18.4\%$). The repair is substantial, rather than complete.

\paragraph{Recovered transfer still relies on source reading.}
After SC at layer $16$, cutting reader-to-source attention from the next
layer reduces accuracy from $42.1\%$ to $7.0\%$. In the unmodified donor,
reader-only and combined cuts at layer $24$ both leave $25.4\%$ correct,
whereas a source-self-only cut leaves $96.5\%$. Late source dependence thus
reflects continued reading, not merely disruption of the source's internal
updates. Earlier, at layers $8$ and $16$, either path cut is damaging.
At layer $24$, recutting after SC gives $27.2\%$, with no detected additional
loss; we therefore locate the repaired-yet-reading-dependent effect at
layer $16$, rather than attributing it to every tested depth.

The $38.6$-point layer-$16$ repair gain remains $38.5$ points on the $96$
items whose three baselines finish without truncation. Even assigning all
truncated S outputs correct and all truncated SC outputs incorrect leaves
a $35.1$-point gain. Skill-group uncertainty, fixed-set estimates and
truncation diagnostics are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}.
