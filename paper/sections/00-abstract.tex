\begin{abstract}
Agent skills provide task-specific capabilities by loading instructions and
supporting resources into a language model's context. When a model applies
a skill successfully, can its internal states be reused in another
execution, and how does that ability relate to continued access to the
skill? We study these questions with two interventions across model depth.
We transplant correct-skill states into a wrong-skill execution and measure
recovered performance. Separately, we block subsequent attention to the
skill in the original execution and measure retained performance. Across
three models from two families on MedCalc and TheoremQA, skill-state
transfer weakens before dependence on source access subsides. Content,
position and cross-question controls establish that early transfer carries
structured skill information. Repeated late replacement leaves transfer
weak, motivating a test of the computational history surrounding those
states. On Qwen3-8B/MedCalc, jointly replacing skill states and their
attention-cache entries raises layer-$16$ accuracy from $3.5\%$ to $42.1\%$.
Cutting subsequent source reading reduces it to $7.0\%$. These findings
separate the ability to use a contextual skill from the ability to reuse
its internal states. A skill can remain important to the original
computation after its states become difficult to transfer, and recovering
transfer can still require continued access to the source.
\end{abstract}
