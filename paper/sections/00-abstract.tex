\begin{abstract}
When a language model follows a newly supplied skill, has it acquired an
internal ability that can be extracted and reused, or does that ability
remain tied to the original source and computation? We investigate this
question through two interventions along model depth. We transplant
correct-skill span states into a wrong-skill execution and measure recovered
performance; separately, we block subsequent attention to the skill in the
original execution and measure retained performance. Across three models
from two families on MedCalc and TheoremQA, skill-state transfer weakens
before source dependence subsides. The ordering persists on common eligible
items. In this intermediate interval, the source remains important even
though its states have little corrective effect in the receiver. Content,
position and cross-question controls establish that early transfer carries
structured skill information, while repeated late replacement does not
restore its effect. Focused experiments on Qwen3-8B/MedCalc show that
repairing source states together with their attention caches raises layer-$16$
accuracy from $3.5\%$ to $42.1\%$, yet blocking subsequent source reading
reduces it to $7.0\%$. Late transfer therefore depends on computational
history, and recovering transfer need not remove source dependence.
These findings separate using a contextual skill from
reusing its internal states: successful application does not imply an
independently reusable internal skill, and failed state transfer does not
mean that the model has finished using the source.
\end{abstract}
