Still Needed, Hard to Transfer: Using and Reusing Contextual
Skills
Anonymous Author(s)
Abstract
Language models can adapt to new tasks by following instructions
provided in context. Reusing the internal states formed from these
instructions could allow the resulting task capabilities to be shared
across executions. However, it remains unclear how the ability to
transfer these states relates to the model’s continued dependence
on the instructions. We study this relationship using skills, sets of
instructions that describe how to perform a task. At different layers,
we transfer states at the instruction positions from an execution
using the correct skill into one using an unrelated skill. Separately,
we block attention to the skill in the original execution. Across four
models and two benchmarks, all eight comparisons show that trans-
fer weakens at later layers while the original execution remains
strongly dependent on the skill. We then augment state transfer
with key/value caches, the stored information used by attention.
At layer 16, this joint intervention improves accuracy over state
transfer alone by 6.8 to 43.4 percentage points across all eight combi-
nations. For Qwen3-8B/MedCalc, accuracy rises from 3.5% to 42.1%,
and blocking subsequent skill reading reduces it to 7.0%. Complete-
source controls further show that donor skill-position KV alone
does not recover donor performance; the depth dependence of joint
repair varies across models and tasks. These findings establish a
separation between using contextual skills and reusing their inter-
nal states, and demonstrate cache-assisted recovery across models
and tasks.

Introduction
Language models can perform new tasks by following instructions
in their context. A skill makes such instructions reusable: a calcula-
tor skill, for example, specifies a formula, input conventions and
the steps for applying them to a new question. A model divides text
into tokens and represents each position with an internal vector,
or hidden state. If the hidden states formed from these instruc-
tions also support reuse, one execution could supply task-relevant
information to another without changing the model’s weights.
Agent Skills organize instructions, scripts and resources around
a SKILL.md entry point [32]. Web agents use reusable skills and
workflows to guide later actions [28, 29]. This motivates studying
whether the internal states formed from such instructions can also
be reused. We study this question through controlled question
answering after a skill has been selected, isolating the use and
transfer of its instructions.
The difficulty is interpreting a failed transfer. If copying a
skill’s hidden states no longer restores performance, has the model
stopped needing those instructions, or has the copy failed to supply
what the receiving execution needs? Task vectors summarize task
demonstrations, while function vectors combine selected internal
attention outputs. Both show that internal states can transmit task
behaviour across contexts [13, 24]. Yet restoring an execution and
disrupting a successful one measure different effects [12]. This

the original execution still relies on access to the skill?
We answer this question with two tests across model depth.
The first copies hidden states at the skill’s token positions from a
correct-skill execution into an execution using length-matched text
from an unrelated skill. The second blocks attention to the skill in
the original correct-skill execution, from a chosen layer onward.
Content, position and cross-question controls first establish that
early copying transfers skill-relevant information. We then find
a consistent separation in eight evaluated combinations of four
models and two benchmarks: transfer weakens at later layers while
the original execution remains strongly dependent on skill attention
(Figure 1).
We next test whether adding information omitted by hidden-
state copying can improve late transfer. Attention stores informa-
tion from previously processed tokens in a key/value (KV) cache. At
layer 16, copying these caches together with hidden states improves
accuracy over state transfer alone in all eight settings, by 6.8 to 43.4
percentage points. Blocking subsequent skill reading reduces the
recovered accuracy in each setting. For Qwen3-8B/MedCalc, joint
replacement raises accuracy from 3.5% to 42.1%, and the reading
cut reduces it to 7.0%. The shared direction across models and tasks
establishes cache-assisted partial restoration alongside continued
use of the skill.
Additional complete-source controls show that copying every
layer of skill-position KV is still insufficient to reproduce the donor.
Copying the complete prefix KV reproduces donor outputs, while
the early-versus-late joint-repair pattern varies across models and
tasks.
The paper follows three steps: validate early transfer (§4.2), estab-
lish its separation from continued skill use (§4.3), and test the extent
of cache-assisted restoration (§4.4). Together, the tests distinguish
the ability to use contextual instructions from the ability to reuse
their internal states, and identify cached information as a compo-
nent that improves reuse.
2
Related Work
State transfer and its interpretation. Task vectors summarize
demonstrations as internal vectors [13], and function vectors
identify attention outputs that transmit task behaviour [24]. Instruc-
tions also yield transferable components [2, 6], whose effects vary
with intervention depth [18]. Activation patching, which copies
internal states between executions, requires matched controls and
careful implementation [12, 33]. In particular, restoring behaviour
and disrupting it need not reveal the same components. Interactions
with other components can also affect a patching estimate [26].
We use these methodological distinctions to compare skill-state
transfer and source dependence across depth.
Depth, source access and context caches. Pola and Balasubra-
manian [20] locate an onset beyond which selected hidden-state
replacements largely cease to change answers in their instruction
tasks. Sia et al. [22] use layer-wise context masking, which blocks
attention to selected context positions, in machine translation
and code generation to locate where attention to the instructional
context becomes unnecessary. Joint transfer and masking analyses
also locate subtask computation [31]. We instead define the source
as an explicit skill-instruction span and compare transfer into
a wrong-skill execution with blocking attention to that span in
the original execution on common questions. The comparison
measures how the two effects separate across depth.
PrefillNotes [16] studies editable and reusable KV caches. In
its field-editing tasks, replacing local KV entries can fail when
downstream cached conclusions still reflect the old field. It also
composes precompiled skill caches into new contexts. Our focus is
the depth separation between skill-state transfer and original-run
source dependence. We then ask whether copying source caches
together with states partially restores late transfer.
Skills and reusable context. Agent Skills organize instructions
and resources for on-demand use [32]. Benchmarks study skill
retrieval and application [11, 17, 23], while matched-budget evalua-
tions expose their dependence on available computation [10]. We
hold skill selection fixed. Other approaches compress context into
aggregated activations [1], learned summary tokens [19], hidden
states [8], or weight updates [3, 4]. Our interventions characterize
naturally occurring states and source dependence without training
a compression mechanism.

Problem Formulation
Skills, executions and token positions. We supply a skill’s instruc-
tions directly and hold skill selection fixed. For the same question,
the donor execution uses the correct skill and the receiver execution
uses length-matched text from a fixed unrelated skill (Appendix A.1).
The skill’s token positions, denoted I, form the source span. For
example, a calculator’s formula and input conventions belong to
this span. The question does not. Readers, denoted J, are positions
after the source span, including subsequent prompt tokens and gen-
erated answer tokens. Thus receiver names an execution, whereas
reader names a token position that can attend to the skill. Causal
attention prevents a position from reading future tokens, so tokens
before the source cannot read it.
Layers are indexed from zero. The model processes prompt
tokens during prefill and generates answer tokens during decoding.
We use greedy decoding, which selects the highest-probability next
token. Our main comparison asks what transferring source states
restores in the receiver and what blocking source attention removes
from the donor.

4
Experiments
The experiments follow three questions. Does early state transfer
carry skill-relevant information? Does that transfer weaken before
dependence on the skill does? Can source-cache replacement restore
part of the late transfer deficit?
4.1
Experimental setup
Tasks and models. We pair MedCalc-Bench [15] with skills from
SRA-Bench, a benchmark that pairs questions with reusable skill
documents [23], covering 1,100 questions from 55 clinical calcula-
tors. We also use SRA-Bench’s TheoremQA subset, with 747 ques-
tions and 320 skills. TheoremQA tests the application of theorems
across mathematics, physics, electrical engineering and computer
science, and finance [5], extending the evaluation beyond clini-
cal calculations. Each question receives its designated skill. Skill
selection is held fixed.
The four main models are Qwen3-8B [30], Mistral-7B-Instruct-
v0.3 [14], Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [9] and DeepSeek-LLM-7B-Chat [7].
The depth study covers all eight combinations of model and bench-
mark (Table 2). Qwen3-8B and Mistral on TheoremQA use regener-
ated answers with independently checked grading (Appendix A.2).
Models generate step-by-step answers without tools using greedy
decoding, and the benchmark scorers evaluate those answers. On
the full MedCalc benchmark, Qwen3-8B achieves 74.2% accuracy
with the correct skill, 34.5% without one, and 31.5% with an unre-
lated skill.
Analysis populations. We select questions using earlier with-skill
and without-skill outcomes, then re-evaluate unmodified runs, or
baselines, under each protocol. The group receiver screen retains a
skill only if its receiver solves none of its sampled questions.
For Qwen3-8B/MedCalc, we use 135 questions for controls
(Table 1), 134 for common depth curves (Figure 3), 124 for the
paired-outcome check (§4.3), and 114 for repair and path cuts
(Figure 4). The control donor answers 124/135 correctly. The com-
mon depth set intersects transfer eligibility with correct-with-skill,
incorrect-without-skill knockout outcomes. The paired check also
requires both donors to be correct.
The added control cells require correct donor and incorrect no-
skill baselines. Repair uses that rule plus an incorrect receiver for
each item. Llama and DeepSeek reuse fixed candidate pools selected
by earlier models. Intervention estimates are conditional on these
selection rules. Each depth panel uses common question IDs for its
two interventions. Repair and path tests likewise share question

4.2
Validation of skill-state transfer
Early transfer recovery. The depth comparison requires a state-
transfer test that works at early layers. At layer 8 on the 135-item
Qwen3-8B/MedCalc set, copying states at the skill positions yields
110 correct answers. The donor answers 124 of the same 135 ques-
tions correctly and the receiver answers none, giving aggregate
recovery 𝜌= 110/124 = 0.89 (Table 1). Patching the last prompt
position or the final 16 positions has much smaller effects. The skill
span is therefore an effective intervention site. The matched-prefix
controls below compare content at equal replacement lengths.
Content and position controls. The paired content controls replace
a common prefix length with either correct-skill or other-skill infor-
mation. Correct content recovers 0.65 of the donor’s gain, versus
0.05 for other-skill content. Shuffling source positions and inject-
ing random vectors of matching length also give little recovery.
The latter is the norm-matched noise control, which preserves the
Euclidean length of the skill-state change. Rescaling the correct
patch to the receiver’s norm still recovers 0.87. Together, these con-
trols support a role for skill-specific content and its arrangement.
The expanded table reports the same controls for every model and
task. Since layer 8 follows the transfer boundary in several cells, its
lower recovery is consistent with Figure 3. An additional layer-2
check gives 56.0% to 93.4% span accuracy across the eight cells,
above noise and shuffled-position controls in each case (Appen-
dix A.2).
Cross-question transfer. In the main prompt order, the skill pre-
cedes the question. Causal attention prevents those skill states from
encoding a later question. We additionally place the skill after the
question and take donor states from other questions using the same
skill. On 151 eligible items from 17 calculators, these states recover
0.98 at layer 8, close to 1.02 for a donor answering the current
question. At layer 16, the values separate to 0.29 and 0.49. Early
transfer thus carries information shared across questions, with
greater question sensitivity later.

separately from the original execution’s dependence on the skill. If

gains and receiving-history effects motivate reuse methods that
account for both source representations and the receiving execu-
tion’s cached state. Deployment studies can evaluate their latency,
memory use and task performance in interactive Web settings.
Future work. A next step is to investigate which questions bene-
fit from cache replacement. The mathematical analysis connects
remaining cache differences to output stability under explicit
assumptions. Measuring these differences, their influence on token
predictions and the donor’s prediction margins could test whether
this framework helps distinguish successful from unsuccessful
repairs. The complete-source controls further motivate testing
which parts of the receiving history must be recomputed, and
whether the required budget can be predicted on held-out skills.
The heterogeneous depth effects suggest that a recomputation rule
should be tested across model/task cells rather than inferred from
one depth curve. Separating attention cuts during prompt pro-
cessing from those during answer generation would clarify when
continued skill access matters. Finally, multi-step Web workflows
would assess how far the observed separation extends beyond the
question-answering settings studied here.
6
Conclusion
Using a contextual skill and reusing its internal states are distinct
capabilities. Across four models and two benchmarks, state trans-
fer weakens before the original execution’s dependence on skill
attention, with half-effect boundaries separated by 8 to 20 layers.
Early content controls establish that the transferred states carry
skill-relevant information, and the depth comparisons show that
this information becomes harder to reuse while the skill remains
useful.
Source-cache replacement provides a way to recover part of that
transfer. At layer 16, adding caches improves accuracy over states
alone by 6.8 to 43.4 percentage points in all eight comparisons, while
cutting subsequent skill reading reduces the joint-repair accuracy
in each. Complete-source controls further show that skill-position
KV alone is insufficient to recover donor performance. Full-prefix
copying reproduces the donor, while the depth effect of joint repair
varies across models and tasks. The mathematical analysis con-
nects cache alignment to output stability under explicit conditions.
Together, these findings motivate evaluating state reuse separately
from continued skill use and including cached information in the
design of reusable skill representations.

A
Experimental protocols and provenance
Tasks and scoring. MedCalc provides 55 calculator skills and 20
questions per calculator through SRA-Bench, scored against pre-
scribed numerical tolerances. TheoremQA uses its released prompts,
skill assignments and evaluator. Skills are supplied directly, with
no tools during generation. Groups are calculators for MedCalc and
skill identities for TheoremQA.
Evaluation populations. The corrected Qwen3-8B/MedCalc trans-
fer pool contains 467 of 470 historically rescued items from 48
calculators. Whole-group screening retains 135 items from 14 cal-
culators, including donor failures: 124 donors and 110 layer-8 span
patches are correct. Recovery uses this measured donor gain. The
common transfer/knockout depth set has 134 items and separate
intervention baselines. The seven added control cells require fresh
donor success and no-skill failure. Repair instead screens individual
items; Appendix B.1 gives its rule. Complete-source controls retain
fixed candidates, including fresh errors (Appendix B.4). These pop-
ulations are not interchangeable. The common DeepSeek/MedCalc
depth set has only 25 items; threshold sensitivity does not establish
robustness to sample composition.

Population and protocol. The eight cells use 580 model–question
pairs, retaining fresh baseline errors (Table 5). Qwen3-8B/MedCalc
reuses all 124 frozen candidates from its 49-condition study. The
other seven cells use pre-existing historical pools, capped at the
first 124 IDs in their original order; one DeepSeek/MedCalc item
fails input-length preflight, leaving 456 new pairs. Selection pre-
cedes repair outcomes. These are selected pools, not full-benchmark
estimates or 580 independent questions across models; they dif-
fer from Table 4’s screened populations. Wrong uses the original
distractor. Neutral repeats or truncates one fixed unrelated docu-
ment to match source positions; it may still influence answers. All
conditions use BF16, eager attention, split prefill, greedy decoding
and 1536 new tokens. TheoremQA resets parser globals per answer.
Layers 8/16/24 are block indices, not matched fractions of model
depth.
Conditions and numerical identities. The 24 conditions comprise
four baselines (donor, Wrong, Neutral, no skill), 𝑆𝐶and 𝑆𝐶exact
at 𝐿8/𝐿16/𝐿24 in both receivers, and four further conditions per
receiver: all-layer source-KV copying, other-position KV copying,
complete-prefix KV copying, and all-layer source copying followed
by replay of the later prompt.𝑆𝐶exact explicitly rewrites every source
K/V tensor after 𝑆𝐶. Runtime elementwise checks find zero source
discrepancies at all layers in every measured 𝑆𝐶condition. The
explicit rewrite preserves all 3,480 generated sequences across the
eight cells. These identities concern KV at skill positions, not all
residual states or all prefix KV. Other-position KV can still encode
effects of earlier skill processing.
Complete-prefix copying reproduces all 1,160 donor sequences.
Replay uses donor source KV and recomputes the later prompt;
both require same-question donor computation. Replay is not exact:
Qwen3-8B/TheoremQA scores 62/63 versus the donor’s 61/63.
Paired uncertainty. Table 6 uses 10,000 whole-skill-group boot-
strap resamples and percentile intervals (seed 20261024 for the
seven new cells; Qwen3-8B/MedCalc reuses its audited analysis).
Intervals are exploratory, unadjusted for multiple comparisons,
and some cells contain few groups. Qwen3-8B/MedCalc’s early-
minus-late gap remains 63.7/64.5 points under adversarial scoring
of capped outputs in Wrong/Neutral.
